# name: Cover letter
# used: When Hal drafts a cover letter for a job
# needs: name, letter_words, tone, spelling, type_letter_mentions, type_evidence, avoid_phrases, voice_examples
Write a cover letter from {name} for the job in the request.
About {letter_words} words, and not much fewer, in three or four paragraphs.
The letter answers what this employer needs: read what they need and
the fit map, choose the two or three pieces of the applicant's evidence that meet those needs best, and
build one argument from them for why {name} can do this job. Mention
{type_letter_mentions}. The evidence that counts most for this applicant is {type_evidence}.
The tone is {tone}; write in {spelling}.
Shape:
- The first paragraph names the role and gives one reason for this employer:
  tie one specific thing about the applicant (a named project, job, module or interest from their facts)
  to one specific thing the employer does (from the employer note), in the applicant's own words.
- The middle is organised by what the employer needs, not by project. Weave the evidence together: one
  paragraph may draw on two pieces of work, and one piece of work may answer two needs. For each piece,
  say what was done, where, the applicant's part, and what came of it.
- Never write one project per paragraph. Never end a paragraph with a sentence saying the work is
  relevant, shows a skill or is useful for this job ("This experience ...", "which is relevant to ...",
  "This shows I can ..."): let the facts make the case.
- Vary how paragraphs open. No two paragraphs start the same way, at most one starts with "I", and none
  starts "I built <project>, a personal project that ...".
- The last paragraph is one or two sentences asking for the next step.
Rules:
- Use only the facts given. Never invent a number, name, date, employer, tool or result; where a result
  has no number, say who it served or what it made possible. The fit map is a guide to what to argue, never
  a fact. Never add a duty, a result, a length of time, or what the work taught or proved, that the facts do
  not state.
- Today's date and the applicant's stage are given. Never write of finishing, completing or graduating from
  a course that has already ended, and never give a start date that has passed.
- Each piece of work says where it was done (degree work, university team project, at work, personal
  project, school work). Never change it: a personal project is never part of a degree, coursework or
  research, a university team project is never a personal project, degree work is never personal work or a
  job, and school work is never degree work, university work or work experience.
- Keep each piece of work's own tools and the applicant's part as given: a tool of one project is never
  another's, and a team's work stays a team's.
- Never restate the advert or the employer note as the reason; name one thing in a few words and say what
  it means to the applicant. The reason must fit no other employer.
- Add what the CV cannot say (context, motive, the link to this job); never copy CV lines.
- Plain words and short sentences. No stock first line ("I am writing to express my interest"), no
  "not just X but Y", no list of three qualities, no clause ending in -ing that says what something
  shows, no long dashes, no hedging, no closing line about being a valuable asset or a great fit.
- Write in the first person, as {name} writing to the employer, without writing round "I" ("The
  dissertation demonstrated ..." reads as if someone else wrote it).
- Follow the conventions for this kind of job when the request gives them.
Never use these words or phrases: {avoid_phrases}. No greeting line or sign-off; those are added
separately. Reply with the letter only.
{voice_examples}
